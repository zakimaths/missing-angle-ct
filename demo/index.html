New to CT? Start here.

A CT reconstruction is a picture of a thin slice through an object, calculated from X-rays measured at different angles. Your task is to discover how much information you need to recover its shape and small details. You can use the built-in samples without coding or uploading a file.

  1. Make your first image. Keep Helsinki object A and the starting settings, then press Reconstruct from views. Watch the blank image fill in. Pause and use One view to see what one more view contributes.
  2. Look closely. Pick a small hole or an edge. Compare your image with the reference below. Is the feature in the same place? Is its outline as clear?
  3. Change one thing. Try 90 views, reconstruct again, and compare. Predict what will happen before you run it. Download each result if you want to keep it for your notes.
A few useful words before you begin

View or projection: a set of X-ray readings taken at one angle. Each reading describes a whole path through the object, so one view cannot tell you exactly where material lies along that path.

Reconstruction: the image calculated by combining these readings. Reference: an image used for comparison. For the real objects, this is the authors’ reconstruction using the full scan; it is a useful benchmark with its own imperfections.

Attenuation: how strongly a material weakens X-rays. Brighter pixels here represent higher calculated attenuation. Artifact: a streak, blur or false feature introduced by the measurement or reconstruction process.

Choosing your settings: “Views to use” is how many angles the calculation receives. Spreading them out gives coverage around the object. With fewer views, using only the first angles leaves a gap in that coverage. Image size controls the number of pixels, so a larger image takes more work to calculate and does not automatically reveal more detail.

Enhance and check the reconstruction

Once the first reconstruction is complete, make more correction passes. Optional light smoothing can reduce grain, but may also soften small details. The image is never replaced with a stored answer. More passes can fit noise: keep the first pass if the comparison errors increase.

What is a correction pass? The method works through the selected views again, checks what X-rays the current image would predict, and adjusts the image where those predictions disagree with the readings. It reuses the measurements you chose; it does not acquire new angles. Try light smoothing and check whether a small hole becomes clearer or starts to disappear.

Measurement agreement will appear when a calculation finishes.

A smaller measurement mismatch does not prove that every feature is correct. Unused views are scored separately and never used to correct the image.

How close is the reconstruction?

Check the first pass against your current image. Lower RMSE and MAE mean smaller errors; bias tells you whether values are too high or too low. Correlation describes a relationship, not accuracy.

What do the error scores mean?

RMSE (root mean square error) summarises the size of the differences and gives larger mistakes extra weight. MAE (mean absolute error) is the average size of a difference. Zero means an exact match for the values being compared.

Bias is the average signed difference: positive means values tend to be too high, negative too low. Opposite errors can cancel, so a bias near zero does not guarantee a good image.

Pearson r describes how closely two sets of values rise and fall together. Even r = 1 can hide a scaling error: predictions that are always twice the readings still move together perfectly. Read it alongside RMSE, the scatter plot and the images.

Selected rays helped build your image. Unused rays were kept out of the calculation, so checking them asks whether your image also explains measurements it did not use. Image errors and ray errors compare different quantities; compare the first-pass and current values within the same row.

Measured versus predicted: paired data

Each dot in the left plot pairs an actual detector reading with the reading your reconstructed image predicts. Dots near the dashed line agree; dots above it are predicted too high, and dots below it too low. In the right plot, high points show angles that the image explains less well. Look for a pattern rather than judging one number alone.

Reading the fitted line and R²

The fitted line summarises the relationship between measured and predicted readings. Perfect agreement would have slope 1 and intercept 0. R² compares prediction errors with simply using the average measured reading: 1 is an exact match, 0 is no better by this measure, and a negative value is worse. It is not the percentage of the anatomy reconstructed correctly.

R² can be negative if predictions are worse than the mean reading. These are descriptive statistics: neighbouring rays are correlated, so no independence-based p-values are reported. Repeatedly tuning on unused views makes them a validation set, not a final blind test.

Try a short investigation
  1. Choose one real object. Use 90 views spread across the scan and reconstruct it. Name the run, mark one small feature, and download the result.
  2. Keep 90 views but choose Use the first angles only. Reconstruct again. The view count is unchanged: what difference does the missing range of angles make?
  3. Enhance this result with three extra passes and light smoothing. Compare the same feature and the error scores. Does a cleaner-looking image also give a smaller error?

For your notes: “I changed ___. I expected ___. I observed ___ in the image and ___ in the error scores. This suggests ___, although ___ is still uncertain.” A useful conclusion explains the evidence, including cases where the scores disagree.

Bring your own projections

Use a JSON file with one sinogram row per angle, angles in degrees, and the scanner's fan-beam or parallel-beam geometry. Values must already be corrected, log-transformed line integrals. Download a test input to see the format. Importing and calculating happen on this device.

The Mac app also accepts the original HTC2022 MATLAB files. Photographs, CT slices and ordinary DICOM image stacks are already images; they do not contain the required raw projection geometry.

Source and what this experiment can tell you

The built-in tests are physical plastic objects scanned at the University of Helsinki. Each has 721 acquired angles from 0° to 360°, including the repeated end orientation. Detector readings are averaged from 560 to 140 bins for this teaching lab. They are measured projections, not simulated views of an existing reconstruction.

The calculation uses a 2D central-plane model, nonnegative SART corrections and optional neighbour smoothing. It is a small educational reconstruction, not a clinical scanner pipeline. HTC2022, Meaney and colleagues (2023), CC BY 4.0.

What should you look for?

  1. Too few views: compare “Many views” with “Fewer views.” Both cover 180°, but sampling is sparser.
  2. Missing angles: compare “Fewer views” with “Missing angles.” Both use 48 views; the second leaves a 60° gap.
  3. Noisy measurements: add noise and compare the correction methods. Which streaks disappear, and which fine details become less distinct?
How do the methods work?

Filtered back projection (FBP) filters each projection and spreads its contribution back into the image. SART starts with a blank image, predicts measurements, and repeatedly corrects the disagreement. The smoothing version applies total-variation (TV) denoising between SART passes, favouring smoother regions while allowing edges. These are mathematical methods; no trained AI model is used.

We preserve the original restricted-angle FBP weighting. The 10-pass version also assumes nonnegative attenuation. See the methods and measured results, including a 10-pass comparison without smoothing.

Where do the images come from?

Eight anonymised slices from one public 3D Slicer CTChest volume. The acquiring hospital and original scanner measurements are not documented. These are existing CT reconstructions, not perfect anatomical ground truth.
